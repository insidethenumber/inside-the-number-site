Why the posted price isn't the real one

Add up the implied probabilities on both sides of any market and you'll get more than 100% — usually 102-105% on a moneyline, far more on props and futures. That surplus is the book's margin: the amount you pay for the privilege of betting.

Dividing each side by the total rescales the market to 100% and reveals the no-vig line — the closest thing to the market's honest opinion. It's the baseline any serious handicapper compares their own number against: beat the fair price, not the posted one.

How to read the result

The calculator gives you two separate answers. The book's margin tells you how expensive the market is. The fair odds tell you where the same market would trade if the sportsbook's cut were removed.

Standard spread
-110 / -110

Both sides imply 52.4%. Together that is 104.8%, so the margin is 4.8 points. Remove it and each side is a true 50%.

Uneven moneyline
-138 / +116

The favorite is not really as expensive as -138 once the margin is stripped out. The calculator converts both sides into one fair market.

What to do with no-vig odds

Start with both sides. A single price does not show the hold. You need the favorite and the underdog, or the over and the under.
Remove the margin. The calculator rescales both implied probabilities so the market adds to exactly 100%.
Compare your number to the fair price. If your own estimate is stronger than the no-vig line, that gap is the price difference worth paying attention to. If it only beats the posted line by a little, the book's cut may be doing the work.

Margin, overround and hold

There are two ways people quote the same fee, and the difference is the most common source of confusion here. Two sides at -110 each imply 52.4%, so the market totals 104.8%. The overround is the 4.8 percentage points above 100 — that is the figure the calculator above reports as the book's margin. The hold is that same surplus as a share of the whole market (4.8 ÷ 104.8), which comes to about 4.5%.

Neither is wrong; they answer slightly different questions. This page uses the overround throughout, so that every number here agrees with the tool at the top. If you are comparing two markets, make sure you know which one the other source is quoting.

Margin is not fixed. It tends to be thinnest on the biggest, most-bet markets and widest where fewer people are betting and the book carries more uncertainty — player props, exotics, three-way markets. That is a statement about market structure, not a measured claim about any particular sportsbook.

Two worked examples

Illustrative — not a real game

A total priced -105 over, -115 under

Over implied   105 ÷ 205 = 51.2%
Under implied  115 ÷ 215 = 53.5%
Market total   104.7% — margin 4.7 points
Fair over / under  48.9% / 51.1%
Fair odds      +104 / -104

Unequal juice on a total is the market leaning before the number itself moves. Here the under is the slightly favoured side at a total both sides nominally share.

Illustrative — not a real game

A heavy favorite at -400, underdog +300

Favourite implied  400 ÷ 500 = 80.0%
Underdog implied   100 ÷ 400 = 25.0%
Market total       105.0% — margin 5.0 points
Fair favorite / dog  76.2% / 23.8%
Fair odds          -320 / +320

The posted -400 implies 80%, but the market's honest opinion is 76.2%. Lopsided markets often carry more margin than even ones, and it is invisible until you take it out.

Common mistakes

  • Comparing your number to the posted price. The posted price includes the fee, so measuring against it makes every side look worse than the market actually rates it. Compare against the fair price.
  • Mixing up overround and hold. 4.8% and 4.5% can describe the same -110/-110 market. Decide which one you mean before comparing two markets.
  • Assuming the fee is constant. Main markets and player props are not priced the same way, and the gap is larger than most people expect.
  • De-vigging an incomplete market. The method needs every outcome. Two of three legs in a three-way market rescales to a number that means nothing.
  • Treating the fair price as a result. It is the market's opinion with the fee removed — a starting point for a comparison, not an answer, and the market can be wrong.

Frequently asked questions

What is vig in sports betting?

Vig (or hold) is the sportsbook's built-in margin: the amount by which both sides' implied probabilities exceed 100%. At -110/-110 the total is 104.8%, so the vig is 4.8%.

How do you remove the vig from odds?

Convert both sides to implied probabilities, add them, and divide each side by that total. The rescaled probabilities sum to 100% and convert back into fair odds.

Why does the no-vig price matter?

It is the market's actual opinion with the fee stripped out. Comparing your own estimate against the fair price, rather than the posted one, is the only way to tell whether a number is genuinely better than what the market thinks.

Is no-vig the same as implied probability?

No. Implied probability is what one posted price says before adjustment. No-vig probability is the market after both sides are normalized back to 100%.

Can I use this for spreads, totals, and moneylines?

Yes. Any two-sided market works: spread, total, moneyline, puck line, run line, props, or anything else with prices on both sides.
